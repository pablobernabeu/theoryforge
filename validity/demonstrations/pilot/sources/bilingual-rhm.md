# Translation asymmetry in late bilinguals: the Revised Hierarchical Model

Kroll and Stewart (1994) proposed the Revised Hierarchical Model to explain why late bilinguals translate words at different speeds in the two directions. It concerns people who learned a second language (L2) after early childhood and for whom the first language (L1) remains the dominant language. This description follows its authors' 2010 review of the model, including the revisions they accepted there.

The model distinguishes three kinds of memory representation: the word forms of the L1, the word forms of the L2 and a system of meanings (concepts) that the two languages share. It combines two earlier ideas, that L2 words reach meaning through their L1 translations and that they reach it directly, into one account of how a learner's vocabulary changes with experience. L1 words have strong connections to meaning. L2 words have weaker connections to meaning, so a learner whose L2 is weak tends to reach the meaning of an L2 word through its L1 translation equivalent. As L2 proficiency grows, the direct connection between L2 words and meaning strengthens and reliance on the L1 translation declines.

From these differences, the authors draw several expectations. Translation from L1 to L2 (forward translation) takes longer than translation from L2 to L1 (backward translation). Forward translation is more likely to pass through meaning, because the L1 word is strongly connected to its concept, whereas backward translation can be completed at the level of word forms, by retrieving the L1 equivalent without access to meaning. Variables that act on meaning should therefore affect forward translation more than backward translation; the authors cite a finding that a semantically driven picture-naming task primed forward but not backward translation. The difference between the two directions should shrink as the bilingual becomes more skilled in the L2. Learners at early stages should activate the L1 translation equivalent when they process L2 words, whereas more proficient bilinguals should not use it to retrieve meaning. The authors add an expectation that they describe as not yet tested: in less proficient learners, the translation equivalent should become active before the meaning of the L2 word is available. Where the L2 has become a bilingual's dominant language, the authors report that translation follows the same pattern with the roles of the two languages exchanged.

The 2010 account qualifies the original model in several ways. The authors accept that even learners with little L2 experience can understand some L2 words directly, so the weak connection between L2 words and meaning is itself asymmetric: going from an L2 word to its meaning is relatively easy, whereas going from a concept to the L2 word, as in speaking or picture naming, is effortful. They stress that the model was always an account of word production and translation, not of word recognition, and that the asymmetry matters more for producing words than for recognising them. The original model did not include the later finding that both languages are active in parallel when a bilingual processes words; the authors argue that it can be added to the model. Difficult items, such as infrequent words or abstract words whose translations share no form with them, can make a skilled bilingual behave like a less skilled one, so the asymmetry can appear in proficient bilinguals when the items are hard. Small, heavily practised vocabularies taught in laboratory training studies may not represent ordinary L2 learning. Finally, the authors note that Green's inhibitory control model explains the same translation asymmetry differently, as the difficulty of suppressing the more dominant L1 word when producing an L2 word.
