# Collaborative cognitive load theory

Kirschner, Sweller, Kirschner and Zambrano (2018) extend cognitive load theory, an account of instructional design, from individual to collaborative learning, whether face to face or supported by computers.

The base theory rests on a view of human cognition in which working memory is severely limited in capacity and duration when it handles new information, whereas organised knowledge retrieved from long-term memory, stored as schemas, meets no known working-memory limit. A main aim of instruction is to help learners store domain-specific knowledge in long-term memory. Such knowledge, for example reading or mathematics, is called biologically secondary: people have not evolved to acquire it, and it usually needs explicit instruction. Biologically primary skills, among them general problem solving, speaking a native language and communicating with others, are acquired without instruction.

Cognitive load is the total working-memory resource that a learning task requires, and it has two sources that add together. Intrinsic load comes from the complexity of the material, defined as element interactivity: the extent to which elements must be processed together in working memory because they interact. Element interactivity depends both on the task and on the learner's expertise, because an expert holds interacting elements as one stored unit. Extraneous load comes from the way information is presented or the task is organised and can be changed by the instructor. The authors do not treat germane load as a third additive source. Studying worked examples improves problem-solving skill more than solving equivalent problems, because it reduces element interactivity and extraneous load. Procedures that help novices can harm more expert learners (the expertise reversal effect).

The extension adds two ideas. First, when people learn together, interacting elements can be distributed across the working memories of group members, which together form a collective working memory larger than any one of them, reducing the load on each. Second, collaboration requires transactive activities, that is communication and coordination, which themselves impose cognitive load. When these activities do not serve learning, for example because they produce errors, conflict or duplication, their load is extraneous; they can also serve learning, as when one member explains a step to another. Group learning is expected to be more effective than individual learning when the material is complex enough to exceed each individual's working memory, and less efficient for simple tasks. The outcome reflects a trade-off between the gain from sharing the processing and the cost of transactive activities, and high costs can cancel the gain.

The authors state that the extraneous load caused by transactive activities is lower when the task provides more guidance and support for collaboration, when members have more expertise in the task domain, when they have collaboration skills, when roles are assigned, when they have experience of coordinating their actions on tasks and when they have worked with each other before. It is higher in larger teams and when knowledge is distributed more unevenly among members. In larger teams, this is partly offset by the larger collective working memory if the task is complex enough.

Collaboration helps when the task exceeds individual working-memory capacity under time limits and members lack relevant prior knowledge. It is unnecessary when most members already hold the relevant knowledge, and it can harm learning when advanced learners work on material they have already learned. The closer a communication channel is to face-to-face interaction, the less load collaboration imposes, because it then draws on biologically primary skills. Although collaborating is itself biologically primary, communicating and coordinating about domain-specific material is expected to impose a heavy load, and learners may need to be taught how to collaborate on such tasks.
