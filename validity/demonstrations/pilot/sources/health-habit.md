# Habit and health behaviour

Gardner (2015) reviews how health psychology uses the term 'habit' and proposes a definition. Habit is a process in which encountering a cue, such as a place, a time, the presence of other people or a mood, automatically generates an impulse to perform a behaviour, as a result of a learned association between the cue and the response. Habits form through repetition of a behaviour in a consistent context: each repetition strengthens the association in memory, alternative responses become less accessible and the context comes to trigger the behaviour without awareness, deliberation or effort. Habit, the process linking cue to impulse, is distinct from the behaviour it may produce.

In dual-process terms, habit belongs to a fast, automatic system that runs in parallel with a slower, deliberate system to which intentions belong. A habitual impulse is one of several momentary influences on what a person does, and it can combine with, or compete against, impulses and inhibitions from other sources, including conscious decisions. Habit therefore need not produce behaviour. People can inhibit habitual impulses when they have enough cognitive capacity and self-regulatory resources; when these are depleted, impulses govern behaviour by default. Forming a strong intention to act against a habit may mobilise those resources and so suppress the habitual response. An impulse that is blocked may be experienced consciously as an urge.

The review sets out three relationships. First, the stronger the habit, the more likely the behaviour is to be performed. Second, as habit strength increases, the relationship between intention and behaviour weakens, so that where intention is weak and habit strong, behaviour follows habit and not intention. Gardner attributes these two hypotheses to Triandis's theory of interpersonal behaviour. Third, repetition of the behaviour in the same context strengthens habit. This growth levels off: once habit strength has peaked, further repetition has little effect, and people differ in how quickly habit grows and in the level it reaches even when they repeat the behaviour equally often. Gardner cites a proposal that repetition strengthens habit more when the behaviour is rewarded. While a habit is forming, motivation prompts repetition and repetition strengthens habit; once the habit is established, it prompts further repetition.

Several further expectations follow. Because habit depends on cues, it is not activated when its cue is absent, so a habit can be automatic yet rarely expressed. In unchanging contexts, habits should remain stable and persist when conscious motivation wanes, so interventions that form new habits are expected to keep their effects after they end, and unwanted habitual behaviours are expected to resist change through deliberation, such as changing intentions. Habits are also expected to override counter-habitual intentions, an expectation that the possibility of inhibition qualifies. Moving people away from the contexts that cue a habit, as when they move house, has been proposed as a way to disrupt it; if the change is temporary, the learned association may survive and the behaviour may return with the old context.

Gardner places limits on these claims. Complex health behaviours, such as cycling to work or exercising in a gym, may be habitual in how they are started, in how they are carried out, or in both. Habits and intentions usually agree, because habits form by repeating intended actions, so the claim that habit overrides intention matters chiefly where the two conflict. Habit is a process within the individual, built on cues that are personal to that individual. The account addresses health-related behaviours such as eating, physical activity, drinking alcohol and taking medication.
